# Research report: What are the current constraints on grid-scale battery storage deployment?

## Executive summary

The constraints best supported by this pass's evidence are grid connection, documented almost entirely through U.S. data, and the concentration of battery supply chains in China. In the United States, 2,600 GW of proposed generation and storage projects were waiting for grid access at the end of 2023, typical waits stretch to five years or more, fewer than a quarter of queued projects are expected to reach completion at current rates, and delay carries quantified costs to consumers. On the supply side, the IEA reports that China processed 70 to 95 percent of global lithium, cobalt, phosphate and graphite in 2024, is projected to remain dominant through 2035, and estimates that full implementation of China's export controls could put $6.5 trillion in annual production outside China at risk. FERC's Order 2023 and Order 2023-A introduced queue reform, including stronger financial and site-control requirements and improved storage modeling, and U.S. queue capacity fell for the first time in 2024, but the evidence does not show the bottleneck resolved. Several constraint families raised in review remain unevidenced in this pass: tariffs and trade barriers, siting, permitting and fire-code compliance, wholesale market eligibility and accreditation rules, non-U.S. connection regimes, equipment lead times, and financing conditions.

## Findings

### Grid connection as the binding timing constraint

Grid connection is the most fully evidenced constraint on grid-scale battery deployment in this pass, although the grid-side evidence is confined to the United States. As of the end of 2023, 2,600 GW of proposed U.S. generation and storage projects were waiting for grid access, with typical wait times stretching to five years or more. Research cited from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory puts the average wait in U.S. interconnection queues at five years and indicates that, at current rates, less than a quarter of the energy projects entering those queues will make it to completion. Solar, wind and grid-scale battery storage make up the vast majority of projects in the queues, and they are held back by a nationwide lack of new transmission capacity to integrate new resources. The queue grew 40 percent year over year in 2022, when more than 1,350 GW of generation and 680 GW of storage were waiting, and one set of figures describes growth from roughly 1,400 GW in 2021 to more than 2,000 GW by 2024 with average waits extending to four years. Aggregated reporting across U.S. ISOs and RTOs places over 2,000 GW in queues, with delays stretching from three to seven years in some markets: in MISO more than 170 GW of solar, wind and storage are awaiting interconnection, with many projects facing delays of four years or more; in PJM, queue reforms in 2022 to 2023 have begun to clear the backlog but more than 60 GW remained under study for 2026; and in SPP the DISIS process still leaves developers with uncertainty over upgrade allocation. The delay is not limited to generators, as Google has reported potential transmission grid connection delays of up to 12 years for new data centers. Much of it stems from the need for new transmission lines, substation upgrades and grid reinforcement studies, with interconnection in many regions taking five to ten years. Battery storage is also presented as a partial mitigant: by smoothing sudden spikes in demand and limiting how much a site appears to pull from the grid at any moment, storage can help data centers move through the queue faster.

### Interconnection cost and the price of delay

The cost of connecting, and the cost of not connecting, is documented in the evidence, though from a narrow base of sources. For every $1 billion in transmission investment that is delayed, consumers lose between $150 million and $370 million in net benefits for each year of delay. The failure to connect new, cheaper generation resources in the PJM market cost consumers an estimated $7 billion in a single capacity auction. On the project side, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory studies show that clean energy projects that withdrew from interconnection queues faced much higher interconnection costs; when interconnection costs become high enough to jeopardize a project's economic feasibility, developers withdraw from the queue. That mechanism turns interconnection cost from a project-level problem into a deployment-rate problem: projects that might have been built are cancelled rather than absorbed, and the queued pipeline overstates what will actually be delivered.

Sources: [11][14]

### Queue reform and its limits

U.S. regulators have moved on the queue process. FERC's Order 2023 mandated interconnection queue reform, adding stronger financial and site control requirements intended to discourage speculative projects and improving the modeling of storage, and Order 2023-A reaffirmed most of the order. The results visible in the evidence are partial rather than decisive. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's annual interconnection review, published in December 2025, found that U.S. interconnection queue capacity dropped for the first time in 2024, and a January 2025 LBNL analysis highlighted the need to tighten the linkages between long-term transmission planning and the project-level interconnection process. Regionally, PJM's 2022 to 2023 transition queue reforms had begun to clear that backlog at the time of the reporting cited here, while more than 60 GW remained under study for 2026. The queue figures themselves disagree on magnitude and date, so none of this should be read as the constraint having been resolved.

Sources: [12][13]

### Critical minerals and supply-chain concentration

Battery deployment also depends on a supply chain whose midstream is heavily concentrated in one country. The IEA reports that China retained its dominance over the midstream and downstream EV and storage battery supply chain in 2024, processing 70 to 95 percent of global lithium, cobalt, phosphate and graphite, and RFF puts China at over 60 percent of global processing of cobalt, lithium and manganese and over 70 percent of global extraction of natural graphite. Looking ahead, the IEA projects that through 2035 China is set to supply over 60 percent of refined lithium and cobalt and around 80 percent of battery-grade graphite and rare earth elements, and that by 2030 it will supply 70 percent of battery-grade manganese sulphate and 75 percent of purified phosphoric acid; analysis of announced projects suggests progress toward more diversified refining supply chains is set to be slow. The IEA states that this high market concentration means there is a risk of significant shortfalls in supply if supply from the largest producing country is disrupted for any reason: full implementation of China's export controls could put an estimated $6.5 trillion in annual production outside China at risk, and a complete disruption of battery-grade graphite trade alone could threaten more than $300 billion in annual production, particularly in electric vehicles, energy storage and battery manufacturing. The trend in the evidence runs toward more exposure, not less: excluding rare earths, the average share of mineral supply held by China rose to 72 percent in 2025 from 70 percent in 2023, the number of mineral tariff categories covered by China's export controls has tripled since 2023, and China and Indonesia accounted for more than three-quarters of the growth in refined supplies of key energy minerals over the past two years. The Congressional Research Service likewise treats lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite and rare earth elements as all highly subject to supply chain risks, which arise at many points from extraction and processing through components to end use.

## Uncertainty and conflicting evidence

Several things should temper confidence in the picture above. The grid-side evidence is U.S.-only: non-U.S. connection regimes were sought in this pass but produced no verified claim or retrieved finding, so the report cannot compare queue regimes across jurisdictions. One claim was contradicted: an assertion that the U.S. queue had swelled to a 2,600 GW backlog as of 2026 with median waits approaching five years was rejected, while a verified claim places 2,600 GW in the queue as of the end of 2023; the same figure therefore circulates with two different dates and should not be quoted without one. Queue magnitude estimates also conflict, with roughly 1,400 GW rising above 2,000 GW between 2021 and 2024 and four-year average waits in one account, against 2,600 GW at end-2023 with waits of five years or more in another. Several central numbers rest on sources this pass flagged low confidence, including novoco.com, energy.gov and utilitydive.com, each scored 0.20, even though the underlying claims were verified; the cost-of-delay figures come from a single secondary source. Six constraint families named in review remain unsupported by any verified claim or finding here: tariffs and trade barriers, including Section 301 duties, FEOC compliance and anti-dumping actions; siting and permitting, including local moratoria and fire-code compliance such as NFPA 855 and UL 9540A; wholesale market rules, including FERC Order 841 eligibility, capacity-market accreditation and co-location rules; grid connection outside the United States; equipment lead times for transformers, inverters and cells; and financing and cost-of-capital constraints such as tax-credit transferability, interest rates and offtake bankability. URLs addressing each of these topics appear in this pass's source list but are all flagged low confidence, and no finding was recorded from them, so they are left as open questions rather than answered. Finally, some steps of the research pass failed and the refinement budget was exhausted before the critic accepted the report, so the gaps listed here may reflect process failure rather than an absence of evidence in the literature.
